## The findigns

Yes. With the data you supplied, I would make the proof-of-concept output evidence-aware rather than a simple WR sort.

There is one unavoidable limitation: only three defenses have directly observed G1 samples ≥1,000 in your table. For several other defenses, however, the aggregate count plus G2/G3 counts lets us recover an approximate hidden G1 sample. For the remaining candidates, we can only establish that the sub-threshold, non-G3 pool looks promising; we cannot honestly manufacture a G1 WR.

So the list below is my best defensible top 10 candidate list for a G1 guild, ranked by expected usefulness and confidence in the evidence, rather than raw WR alone.

Why #1 and #2 are so interesting

These are the most interesting findings in the whole exercise.

Morris / Orion / Trevor

You gave:

ALL: 6,343 battles @ 16.2%
G2: 1,672 @ 19.0%
G3: 3,687 @ 12.2%

Therefore:

$$ 6343-1672-3687=\mathbf{984} $$

So there are approximately 984 G1 battles that don't appear in the G1 table because they fall just below the 1,000-battle threshold.

Using the displayed percentages, the implied G1 WR is:

$$ \mathbf{\approx26.4\%} $$

That's remarkable compared with the 20–21% directly observed for the three defenses that make the G1 table.

The estimate is approximate because SWGT shows the component WRs rounded to one decimal place, but the rounding uncertainty only moves the implied value to approximately 25.8–27.0%.

So this isn't merely:

"The aggregate WR is high."

We actually have fairly strong evidence that this defense just missed the G1 table and may have a considerably higher G1 WR than any defense in your visible G1 sample.

That's exactly the kind of thing the 1,000-battle censoring makes easy to miss.

Morris / Eshir / Reno

Same trick:

$$ 6035-1653-3896=\mathbf{486} $$

So approximately 486 battles belong to the hidden G1 bucket.

The implied G1 WR is approximately:

24.9%

with rounding-induced bounds of roughly 23.8–26.1%.

That is potentially fantastic for G1, but the evidence is much weaker than Orion/Trevor because 486 observations is substantially smaller.

The important distinction is:

"Potentially 25% in G1" is not the same evidential statement as "25% over 10,000 G1 battles."

That's why I put it behind Orion/Trevor despite the very attractive estimated WR.

An important finding from this example

I think this proof of concept exposes a fourth category that the handoff should explicitly mention.

We have:

Category A — Direct G1 evidence

The defense has ≥threshold G1 battles.

Examples:

Rex / Shumar
Fiona / Eshir / Liesel
Liu Mei / Vigor

These are the safest statistics to use.

Category B — Recoverable hidden G1 evidence

The aggregate, G2 and G3 counts allow us to calculate:

$$ N_{G1}=N_{ALL}-N_{G2}-N_{G3} $$

Examples:

Orion / Trevor: 984
Eshir / Reno: 486
Gamir / Rex: 197

This is extremely valuable because it tells us about defenses that just failed to reach the G1 display threshold.

But the WR must be treated as approximate because the displayed rank/aggregate percentages are rounded.

Category C — Recoverable combined sub-threshold evidence

For example:

Mihyang / Cichlid / Hraesvelg

ALL:

2,642 @ 14.6%

G3:

2,298 @ 13.2%

Therefore:

344 battles belong to G1 + G2

with an implied combined WR around 24.0%.

We do not know whether that is:

G1 27% + G2 15%

or

G1 20% + G2 20%

or something else.

So it's an interesting signal, but not a G1 statistic.

Category D — Aggregate-only candidate

For example:

Fiona / Eshir / Truffle — 1,794 @ 19.6%

None of its individual rank samples reach the display threshold.

Therefore we can't determine its G1 performance from what you've given me.

But it deserves investigation because almost 1,800 battles produced a 19.6% aggregate WR despite the battles being distributed below the rank-specific display threshold.

One defense I would deliberately NOT put in the top 10

This is useful for demonstrating how the framework prevents misleading conclusions.

Solveig / Cichlid / Molly

Aggregate:

10,722 @ 8.1%

G2:

1,359 @ 10.4%

G3:

9,018 @ 7.6%

Hidden G1:

$$ 10722-1359-9018=345 $$

The implied hidden G1/G2 residual is only about:

12.1%

So even though there is hidden G1 data, there isn't evidence suggesting an unusually strong G1 result.

This is exactly the kind of defense a simplistic "look at aggregate sample size" system could accidentally elevate.

What I think the proof-of-concept has demonstrated

The most important result isn't actually the list of ten.

It's this:

A naïve G1-only analysis misses potentially excellent defenses that sit just below SWGT's 1,000 G1-battle display threshold.

And:

A naïve ALL-ranks analysis can dramatically undervalue defenses that perform especially well in G1 because G3 contributes most of the aggregate observations.

Your two examples make that extremely clear:

Defense	G1	ALL
Morris / Orion / Trevor	~26.4% inferred	16.2%
Morris / Liu Mei / Vigor	20.0%	12.2%
Fiona / Eshir / Liesel	20.1%	11.6%

So an AI agent that simply says:

"Sort ALL by WR"

would give you a materially different—and poorly targeted—answer.

An agent that simply says:

"Filter G1 and sort by WR"

would also miss Orion/Trevor and Eshir/Reno, which appear to have very interesting hidden G1 performance.

The correct workflow is therefore:

Aggregate → identify candidates → reconstruct hidden rank information where mathematically possible → direct G1 evidence → sample-size uncertainty → G2/G3 robustness → final shortlist.
